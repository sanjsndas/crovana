Reading a Modern Concrete House: Form, Structure, and the Cliff Site

When a house is built into an extreme landscape—between rock faces, on a slope, against a cliff—the architecture has a choice: compete with the site or absorb it. The modern concrete house with glass walls that anchors this journal’s featured study takes the second path. Its lessons are transferable to far less dramatic sites.

The Role of Concrete as a Finish, Not Just a Structure

Concrete is often understood only as a structural material—something hidden behind plaster or cladding. In a house of this type, however, concrete is the interior and exterior surface. This decision has consequences. Concrete walls have thermal mass: they absorb heat during the day and release it slowly, which stabilizes interior temperatures in climates with significant diurnal swings. They also have weight, visually and acoustically. A concrete room sounds different from a drywall room. It reflects sound in a way that can feel severe, which is why such interiors almost always soften themselves with timber, wool, and upholstery.

Glass as an Environmental Filter, Not Just a View

Floor-to-ceiling glass in a house facing a cliff is not primarily about the view. It is about light. In a deep-plan concrete structure, glass is the only source of daylight for the living spaces. This means the glazing must be specified carefully: low-iron glass to avoid the green tint of standard float glass, solar control coatings on west and south exposures, and in many cases external shading in the form of overhangs or louvers. The difference between a glass wall that feels luxurious and one that feels like a greenhouse is almost entirely a matter of how the solar gain is managed.

The Plan: How Rooms Organize Around a Central Idea

The plan of a successful concrete house is usually simple. Complexity in the section—the way the building steps with the slope, or frames the cliff through a double-height volume—does the architectural work. The plan itself may be a straightforward sequence of rooms arranged along a single loaded corridor or around a central void. When the materials are heavy and the site is dramatic, the plan does not need to be clever. It needs to be calm.

This is the opposite of what many custom homes attempt. They pursue complexity in both plan and section, and the result is a house that feels busy rather than rich. A single strong spatial idea, executed with consistency, produces a more powerful experience than five mediocre ideas competing for attention.

The Interior Design Principles That Actually Survive Changing Taste

Every five years, the furniture and colors that were "essential" become evidence of a past era. But certain principles remain stable across decades. These are the ones worth building a home around.

1. Light Before Color

No paint color can compensate for insufficient daylight. The single most impactful interior design decision is not the color of a wall but the orientation of a room, the size of its windows, and what hangs in front of them. Before choosing finishes, track the light: where does the morning sun land? Where does the late afternoon light turn harsh? Which rooms never receive direct light and need to be treated differently? Only after answering these questions does color become a meaningful choice.

2. Negative Space Is Not Wasted Space

A room filled with furniture is a room that has stopped being designed. The empty areas—the floor between the sofa and the wall, the surface of a table with a single object—do the same work that white space does in typography. They create hierarchy. They allow the things you do place to be seen. This is not minimalism as an aesthetic. It is clarity as a practice.

3. Texture Over Pattern

Patterns date quickly because they are tied to specific visual trends. Textures age more slowly because they engage touch and light rather than pure visual recognition. A room layered with linen, wool, raw timber, and matte ceramic will feel richer after ten years than a room defined by a graphic wallpaper that was in every design magazine the year it was installed.

4. Circulation Defines Comfort

A beautiful room that you have to walk through to reach the kitchen is a corridor with furniture. The most important spatial quality in a home is not the size of individual rooms but the ease of moving between them. This is why a well-planned 900-square-foot apartment can feel more comfortable than a poorly planned 2,000-square-foot house.

A Practical Guide to Materials That Improve With Age

Some finishes look best the day they are installed. Others look better after five years. For residential interiors, the second category is almost always the better investment.

Oak and Other Hardwoods

Solid oak, ash, and walnut develop a patina over time. Small scratches blend into the grain rather than standing out as defects. Oil-finished floors can be spot-repaired without sanding the entire surface. The key is to avoid aluminum-oxide factory finishes that create a plastic-like film; they are durable in the short term but impossible to maintain in the long term.

Natural Stone

Honed limestone and marble that are allowed to patina—rather than sealed into a state of artificial perfection—are among the most forgiving surfaces in a home. A water mark on a sealed marble counter is a crisis. A water mark on an unsealed or lightly sealed honed stone is simply part of its history.

Lime Plaster and Clay Paint

Mineral finishes like lime plaster and clay paint are breathable, repairable, and visually soft. They reflect light differently from acrylic paint, producing a depth that no flat latex can replicate. They also develop character—small cracks and variations in tone that read as evidence of a real material rather than a manufactured surface.

What to Avoid

Materials that imitate other materials tend to age poorly. Vinyl that mimics wood. Ceramic tile printed to look like marble. Laminate with a photorealistic stone pattern. These surfaces are at their best on installation day and decline from there. An honest, less expensive material—real cork, real linoleum, real pine—will often outlast and out-charm a synthetic imitation of something more "premium."

A Room-by-Room Guide to Making Better Decisions

The Living Room

Start with a rug. The rug defines the conversation area more effectively than any arrangement of furniture. A rug that is too small makes even a large room feel cramped; a rug that is large enough to fit at least the front legs of all seating creates a room within a room. From there, arrange seating around a fixed point—a fireplace, a window, a view—rather than floating it in the center of the floor. The television, if there is one, should be accommodated rather than centered. A room organized around a screen is a room that has given up its potential.

The Kitchen

The triangle—sink, stove, refrigerator—remains the best organizing principle for a kitchen. Everything else follows from it. Counter depth, aisle width (minimum 42 inches between parallel counters, 48 inches preferred), and landing space next to the refrigerator and stove matter more than cabinet door style or backsplash material. A kitchen with excellent layout and modest finishes will outlive a kitchen with spectacular finishes and a bad layout.

The Bathroom

Ventilation and light come first. A bathroom without a window needs mechanical ventilation that actually works, not just a fan that makes noise. Then consider the shower: a walk-in shower with a linear drain eliminates the curb, which makes the room feel larger and easier to clean. Tile selection should prioritize slip resistance over pattern. The floor tile you choose because it looks good in a showroom may be the same tile that sends someone to the hospital after a shower.

The Bedroom

Light control is the primary design problem. Blackout capability is not a luxury; it is a functional requirement. Beyond that, reduce the number of surfaces that collect objects. Every horizontal surface in a bedroom—nightstands, dressers, window sills—will accumulate clutter. Fewer surfaces means less clutter. This is not about discipline; it is about design.

Frequently Asked Questions

  • Interior design typically concerns finishes, furniture, and the visual arrangement of existing spaces. Interior architecture involves changing the spatial structure itself—removing or adding walls, altering ceiling heights, reconfiguring circulation. In residential projects, the two overlap considerably, which is why the best results often come from a single designer or a tightly coordinated team.
  • Costs vary dramatically by region, scope, and finish level. As a rough planning figure in many Western markets, a cosmetic renovation (paint, floors, fixtures) might fall in the range of $150–$300 per square foot, while a structural renovation involving new kitchens, bathrooms, and circulation changes can exceed $500 per square foot. These are planning ranges, not quotes. The only reliable number comes from a contractor who has visited the actual property.
  • If the work involves structure, changes to the building envelope, or modifications that affect how the house performs thermally or acoustically, an architect or structural engineer is often required by regulation and almost always advisable in practice. For purely cosmetic updates—paint, furniture, removable fixtures—an interior designer or a knowledgeable homeowner can manage the work without architectural involvement.
  • Comfort comes from factors that photographs do not capture: thermal quality, acoustic softness, the absence of glare, and the ease of moving through the space. A room that looks beautiful in a magazine can feel cold, echoing, and awkward to use. The most comfortable rooms tend to have layered lighting, soft floor coverings, at least one very comfortable seat, and enough empty space that movement does not require navigating around furniture.
  • Choose materials that are what they appear to be. Real wood, real stone, real wool, real linen, real concrete. Avoid materials that imitate other materials. Avoid surfaces with strong graphic patterns that are tied to a specific visual trend. The safest long-term choices are simple, honest, and slightly imperfect—they have the capacity to develop character rather than merely degrade.
  • Improving daylight. This can mean removing a wall, enlarging a window, adding a skylight, or simply clearing obstructions from existing windows. No other single intervention affects how a room feels, how it is used, and how good it looks in both sunlight and artificial light as reliably as increasing the amount and quality of natural light.

About Crovana Form House

Crovana Form House is an independent editorial journal about interior design and architecture. It is written for people who want to understand not just what looks good, but why certain spaces work—and why others, despite appearances, do not.

The journal publishes long-form articles, practical guides, and case studies. Its editorial position is simple: form follows function, materials should be honest, light matters more than color, and the best interiors are the ones that are easiest to live in.
